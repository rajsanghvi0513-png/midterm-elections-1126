The call

The desk's own opinion, labelled as such. The sourced data is in the sections below and does not depend on it.

Democrats win the House, and the Senate is a real contest that leans their way. Both parties agree on the first half of that sentence; the only Republican argument left for the House is that the polls are broken again. On the Senate I am less sure than the markets. I put Democrats at about 62% to reach 51 seats, against 74 to 78% on Kalshi and Polymarket and 81% at Silver Bulletin, and close to Decision Desk HQ (65%) and CNN's Harry Enten (71%).

The path is specific. North Carolina is gone for Republicans: Roy Cooper leads Michael Whatley by double digits and the Senate Leadership Fund stopped advertising there in early October. Ohio leans Sherrod Brown by about three. Maine leans Troy Jackson, but Susan Collins beat her final polls by roughly eight points in 2020, so I hold that at 60%, not 73%. Alaska leans Mary Peltola and will not be counted until the ranked-choice tabulation on 18 November. Texas is a coin flip that I tilt to James Talarico only because Ken Paxton is a uniquely damaged nominee in a D+9 year; the state's polls overstated Democrats by about three points in 2020 and 2024. Kansas and Iowa I tilt Republican despite small Democratic poll leads, because both states voted for Trump by 13 to 16 points and both have a late-breaking Republican turnout pattern. Michigan, Georgia, New Hampshire and Minnesota hold for Democrats.

Add that up and the most likely single outcome is 51 or 52 Democratic seats. A 50–50 Senate, which Vance breaks for Republicans, is about one chance in nine. Democrats at 54 or more, which needs Kansas or Iowa, is about one in four.

Why I trust the direction more than the polls. Every independent signal points the same way as the polls: Trump's approval is 38 to 39%, the second-lowest for any president entering a midterm since 1946; Democrats have beaten the 2024 presidential baseline by an average of about 13 points across every special election since January 2025; consumer sentiment is at its second-lowest final reading on record; petrol is $4.29, the highest of any Election Day. Midterm polls in 2018 and 2022 were close to unbiased; the big misses of 2016, 2020 and 2024 came when Trump was on the ballot and pulled out low-propensity voters who skip midterms. And the pollsters have already moved: the Times/Siena weighting now produces results more than three points redder than its old method.

Why I still hedge. Republican Senate candidates have beaten their final polls by an average of 4.1 points across 2018 to 2024. Five of the seven Times/Siena Senate leads are inside the margin of error. White Democrats are still 30% more likely to answer a poll than white Republicans. And the markets, which some read as independent confirmation, moved from Republicans 60% in July to Democrats 77% today by following the same polls; they had Flávio Bolsonaro at 80% in Brazil last week, and he lost.

Special elections since January 2025

Democrats improved on Harris's 2024 margin in every House special election held under Trump (Newser, 8 Apr 2026). The Downballot puts the average overperformance at 13 points; MultiState's state-legislative tally shows a median of 10.4 points in January–April 2026 and 10 points in May–October, so the edge has not faded the way it did through 2018 (14.7 early, 0.2 by autumn). In November 2025 Spanberger won Virginia by 15, Sherrill won New Jersey by 13, and the polls had underestimated Democrats by 6.1 points (Silver Bulletin). The caveat, from NPR's Steve Fowler: specials reward the party whose voters show up outside November, and midterm turnout is higher, so some of this edge shrinks.

How the polls have actually missed

Signed bias of the final polling averages, in points, by cycle: Silver Bulletin (14 Jan 2026) with AAPOR task-force figures where they differ. Positive = polls overstated Democrats. Presidential years marked.

The pattern a sceptic should hold onto: the three big misses toward Democrats were all presidential years, and the two midterms in the Trump era were close to unbiased (2018 overstated Republicans by 0.5; 2022 overstated Democrats by 0.8 and the "red wave" never came). The counter-evidence is Senate-specific and real: Enten finds Republican Senate candidates beat their final polls by 4.1 points on average from 2018 to 2024, and Inside Elections' Nathan Gonzales says plainly "there's a chance that the polls are underestimating Republican support". Nate Cohn's last word before the vote: there is "no reason to assume that 2016 or 2020 is the worst-case scenario."

What the pollsters changed

  • Times/Siena's new weighting makes its 2026 Senate polls more than 3 points redder than the old method: Michigan D+16 → D+11, Kansas D+10 → D+5, Ohio D+11 → D+6 (Cohn, 2 Nov).
  • Three in four state polls now weight by education, against one in four in 2016. Recalled-vote weighting is standard; Silver calls it "akin to cheating", Schaffner defends it.
  • Nonresponse is unsolved: white Democrats about 30% likelier to respond than white Republicans in the final Times/Siena polls; the Washington Post's Kansas poll got 32% of Democrats and 12% of Republicans to answer and said so.
  • No 2022-style flood of partisan polls into the averages this year (Washington Monthly, 27 Oct); Republican-aligned firms mostly align with independents, Iowa excepted.
  • Silver's own framing of the Republican case: "Republicans' best hope is that the polls suck again." His model treats a miss in either direction as roughly equally likely.

Prediction markets are not a second opinion

Kalshi has taken over $1.2 billion on midterm contracts, more than it did in the 2024 presidential year, and Polymarket's House and Senate control markets carry $18M and $9.4M. But markets aggregate the same polls, forecasts and news. In 2022 Betfair had a Republican Senate at 78% on Election Day and 12% the next morning. The 2026 Senate market moved 37 points in four months. Treat the prices as a clean read of consensus sentiment, with real money behind it, and nothing more.

The Republican case, as Republicans make it

  • The polls are broken again. RedState's Adam Turner expects Democrats to be overstated by ~2 nationally and 6+ in Iowa, Michigan and Ohio; he points at AtlasIntel's all-adults sample and Times/Siena response rates.
  • Turnout, not persuasion. NRCC chair Richard Hudson: "if Republican voters turn out, we're going to hold the House"; ~30 seats "virtually tied"; Republicans have "the money", "better candidates" and new maps.
  • The maps. Roughly R+9 on paper across TX, FL, OH, NC, TN, LA and AL, net of California and Utah.
  • The economy is stronger than 2006. Hugh Hewitt: 3.4% Q3 GDP against 1.6% in 2006; a war the US is "decisively winning"; 30+ Trump October appearances against Bush's 16.
  • Money and candidates. $150M+ of super-PAC money behind Paxton; Hewitt rates Jackson, El-Sayed and Peltola as weak nominees.
  • The Democratic brand is weak (44/55 Fox, 38/58 WSJ); Heidi Heitkamp: voters are "rejecting the party of Donald Trump," not choosing Democrats.
  • Early vote is "spooky but not fatal" (DDHQ): Election Day voters now lean Republican; Iowa votes mostly on the day; Florida shows a Republican early-vote lead.

Weak points, for balance: the Latino and young-male swing to Trump looks to be reversing (Axios, CNBC on South Texas); early-vote party splits are worse for Republicans than in 2024 in NC, IA, KS, GA and TX (DDHQ); Cook says the seats that swung most toward Trump have the most room to "zoom back".

The Democratic case, as Democrats make it

  • Approval at the second-lowest midterm level since Truman; cost-of-living approval 25/70.
  • Specials: +13 average, improvement in every House special, a 25-point swing in GA-14; 2025 polls underestimated Democrats by 6.
  • Enthusiasm: 46% of Democrats vs 30% of Republicans "very enthusiastic" (Reuters/Ipsos); 63 vs 38 "more motivated" (KFF).
  • Prices, petrol, tariffs: CPI 3.7%, $4.29 petrol, ~$1,100 per household in tariffs; 65% blame Trump's policies for rising prices (AP-NORC).
  • Health care: the enhanced ACA subsidies expired and marketplace enrolment fell by more than a million; health care ties the economy as the top issue and Democrats lead on it 42–22 (KFF).
  • Iran: 47% call the war extremely important; 23 US service members killed since February (Hewitt's own figure).
  • One-way ratings: Sabato made 66 moves toward Democrats and 2 toward Republicans since 1 Oct; Inside Elections 63 to 1 in its final week; Republicans pulled money from North Carolina and Miami.

Democratic worries, in their own words: Obama told donors 2–3 point leads are "within the margin of error"; strategist Doug Gordon, "every Democrat should be running as if they're 10 points behind"; Axios, 3 Nov: expectations so high "some Democrats fear disappointment is inevitable."
