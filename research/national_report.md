# National Environment and Polling Reliability: Election Day, 3 November 2026

Compiled the morning of Election Day, before polls closed. Each figure carries its source and date. Where a number could not be sourced in this session it says "not found" or "not verified". Section 7 lists every URL.

**Bottom line for a poll skeptic.** Every independent measure points the same way: approval, the generic ballot, the special elections, the 2025 off-year results, consumer sentiment, the forecasters and the markets. The polls are not the only evidence of a Democratic year. The question that really matters tonight is *how big* the Democratic year is, and especially whether Democrats win the Senate. That question does sit inside normal polling error. Five of the seven Senate leads in the final NYT/Siena polls are within the margin of error.

### Direction of the country

- Right direction 30%, wrong track 65% (Ballotpedia, end of Oct 2026). The low was 24% / 66% on 2 Sep 2026.
- Congressional approval: 25% (Ballotpedia). Enten cites 14%.

### Economy voters feel

- **Inflation:** CPI rose 3.7% year on year in September (ABC News, 23 Oct 2026; Hewitt also uses 3.7%). The October CPI had not been released as of 3 Nov.
- **PCE price index:** rose 2.1% annualized in Q3, down from 5.0% in Q2. Core PCE was 2.5% (BEA, 29 Oct 2026).
- **GDP:** Q3 real GDP grew at a **3.4% annualized rate**, up from 2.2% in Q2 (BEA advance estimate, 29 Oct 2026). This is the strongest data point Republicans have.
- **Jobs:** payrolls rose just **29,000** in September and unemployment rose to **4.2%** (CNBC headline, 2 Oct 2026; ABC confirms the 29k). The October jobs report is not out until after the election.
- **Gas:** national average **$4.29/gal**, up about 43% since the Iran war began in late February. Oil topped $116 a barrel (ABC, 23 Oct). The Washington Post (2 Nov headline) says gas is set to be the highest of any Election Day in US history. Prices dipped slightly in the final days amid Iran talks (Spectrum / Newscord, 2 Nov).
- **Consumer sentiment:** the final October University of Michigan index was **48.0**, the second-lowest final reading on record. The record low was 44.8 in May 2026 (ABC, 23 Oct). Newsweek (11 Oct) reports sentiment is down 33% since January 2025.
- **Mortgage rate:** 30-year about 7.25% (Hewitt, 3 Nov).

### Tariffs

- On **20 Feb 2026** the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in *Learning Resources v. Trump* that the IEEPA does not authorize tariffs.
- The White House replaced them with a **10% global Section 122 surcharge** (effective 24 Feb, 150-day limit) (Honigman).
- When that expired on **24 July 2026**, it was replaced by new **Section 301 tariffs** of 10–12.5% on more than 80 economies. Yale Budget Lab (24 Jul 2026) puts the average statutory rate at **11.1%**, rising to 11.8% by year-end, at a cost of about **$1,100 per household per year** and an ultimate price-level effect of about 0.7%.
- AP-NORC found 65% blame Trump's policies for rising prices (via Dunham, 2 Nov).

### Late-breaking and structural events

- **Iran war:** began late February 2026. Trump said on 8 Oct that the US would not attack Iran before the election (CNBC). Hewitt cites 23 US service members killed since February and a naval blockade. Talks were continuing in the final days (Newscord, 2 Nov). Trump called the gas price spike "a little glitch" (Yahoo).
- **Shutdowns:** the record 43-day shutdown ran in Oct–Nov 2025. **There was no 2026 shutdown.** A continuing resolution funds the government through 11 Dec 2026, and the House passed it 370–48 (CREFC, 8 Sep 2026).
- **Redistricting and *Callais*:** on 29 Apr 2026 the Supreme Court narrowed VRA Section 2 in *Louisiana v. Callais* (Votebeat, SCOTUSblog).   - Kondik (July) said the redistricting gain alone is "very likely not enough" to decide control.
- **Trump's closing message:** delivered at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool (NBC live blog, 2–3 Nov).
- **Epstein:** a running issue through 2025–26 (Ms. Magazine, Sep 2026). No final-week polling shows it as a top-tier vote driver. Gallup does not list it.

### What it predicts

- **The cautionary 2018 precedent (MultiState):** in 2018, special-election overperformance was 14.7 points early in the year, fell to 0.2 by autumn, and averaged 5.3 points. Democrats then beat the 2016 baseline by 3.9 points in November. In 2026 the overperformance has **not** faded the same way, though the late sample is only six races.
- **Silver Bulletin:** Trump-era off-year and special-election polling has been biased toward Republicans by an average of 1.3 points.
- **Enten:** since 2006, the party with higher aggregate primary turnout has won the House in every midterm (31 Oct). The 2026 primary turnout totals themselves were not found.

**Caveat (Steve Fowler via NPR):** specials reward the party of high-propensity voters. *"Democrats are now the party of folks that are more likely to show up outside of those big November elections."* Midterms have higher turnout than specials, so some of this edge shrinks.

## 3. Polling accuracy: the honest record

### Cycle by cycle

Signed bias is shown as the party the polls overstated (Silver Bulletin, 14 Jan 2026; AAPOR 2024 Task Force, Oct 2025).

| Cycle | Bias | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **2016** (pres.) | Overstated D by 3.0 | National polls were fair. Upper-Midwest state polls missed, largely because many did not weight by education (Schaffner, Boston Globe, 30 Oct 2026) |
| **2018** (midterm) | Overstated R by 0.5 (AAPOR: D by 0.1) | Essentially unbiased. Cohn: final Times/Siena polls were "dead-on" |
| **2020** (pres.) | Overstated D by 4.7 (AAPOR: 4.6) | The worst miss in decades. Average absolute error was 5.3 points (AAPOR). Times/Siena underestimated Trump by 5 points |
| **2022** (midterm) | Overstated D by 0.8 (AAPOR: overstated R by 0.6) | Small error either way. The "red wave" did not come |
| **2024** (pres.) | Overstated D by 2.9 (AAPOR: 2.7 across all offices) | National error 2.6; state presidential error 3.0, "most accurate for any presidential cycle since 1944" (AAPOR). Third straight presidential underestimate of the GOP |
| **2025** (off-year) | Overstated R by 6.1 | Big Democratic overperformance |

### 2022 in detail

- FiveThirtyEight still gave Republicans 59% for the Senate. Betfair had a GOP majority at 78% on Election Day (Pickles / The Conversation, Nov 2022).
- Washington Monthly (27 Oct 2026) says Republican-aligned pollsters lowballed Democrats in AZ, GA, NV and NH, *but nonpartisan pollsters did too*. The clear divergences were:
  - **Washington:** Trafalgar had Murray up 1.7; she won by about 15.
  - **Pennsylvania:** GOP-affiliated polls had Oz winning; Marist had Fetterman +6; Fetterman won by 5.
- In September 2022 Nate Cohn warned of a "blue mirage", arguing Democratic leads sat where polls had overestimated Biden. Fetterman won anyway; Ron Johnson held Wisconsin narrowly. Trafalgar's Robert Cahaly predicted GOP turnout "will exceed even what we predict" (Fox, Sep 2022). It did not.

### Is the "polls underestimate Republicans" pattern a presidential-year effect?

The evidence says mostly yes:
- **Silver Bulletin:** the Democratic-direction bias "has mostly appeared in presidential years". It attributes this to low-propensity Trump voters who show up when Trump is on the ballot and whom polls struggle to reach.
- **Cohn (Inquirer, 2 Nov 2026):** polls have "a good midterm record but a poor presidential-year record in the Trump era."
- **AAPOR:** both 2018 and 2022 had small signed errors.
- **Counterpoint, Enten (31 Oct):** **Republican Senate candidates outperformed final polling by an average of 4.1 points in 2018–2024.** This is Senate-specific and includes two presidential years, but it is the single most relevant caution for tonight's Senate map.
- **Counterpoint, Gonzales (Inside Elections, 31 Oct):** "there's a chance that the polls are underestimating Republican support."

### The specific 2026 debate: over-correction or still missing Trump voters?

**Nonresponse is as bad as ever.**
- In the final Times/Siena battleground polls, registered white Democrats were about **30% likelier to respond** than white Republicans, slightly worse than in 2020 (Cohn, 2 Nov).
- The Washington Post's Kansas poll got about 32% of Democrats versus 12% of Republicans to respond. The Post itself said there was "good reason to suspect the poll overestimates Democratic candidates' support" (Hewitt, 3 Nov; Yahoo).
- Response rates are under 2–3% even for gold-standard polls. The September Siena poll made more than 85,000 calls for 1,503 interviews (Schaffner).

**Weighting is doing the work, and it is cutting toward Republicans.**
- Times/Siena's newer weighting method makes its 2026 polls **redder** than the old method by more than 3 points on average:

| State | Old weighting | Published |
|---|---|---|
| Michigan | D+16 | D+11 |
| Kansas | D+10 | D+5 |
| Ohio | D+11 | D+6 |

  (Cohn, 2 Nov.) In other words, pollsters are already correcting toward the GOP. Cohn says the new method would have improved 2024 by about 1.6 points. He also warns that weighting "is not a panacea": a poll can have the right *number* of Trump voters but the wrong *kind*.
- **Over-correction risk:** Silver Bulletin (Jan 2026) says pollsters "may have overcorrected after three straight presidential-year misses". It points to 2025, where weighting to redder past electorates did worse than modeled bluer ones (State Navigate).
- **Recalled-vote weighting** is now standard. About three in four state polls weighted by education in 2024, versus one in four in 2016. Silver has called party weighting "akin to cheating." Schaffner defends it (Boston Globe, 30 Oct).
- **Herding:** AAPOR found **no evidence** of firms adjusting results to match competitors in 2024. Wider use of political weighting variables did shrink the spread between polls.
- **Partisan flooding:** Washington Monthly (27 Oct) finds no 2022-style flood so far. GOP-aligned firms (Trafalgar, Rasmussen, InsiderAdvantage, co/efficient) "mostly align" with independents. Iowa is the exception.

**Final 2026 polls versus the specials and off-years:**
- The generic ballot is about D+9.
- Specials averaged roughly 10–16 points of Democratic overperformance against 2024.
- The 2025 off-years ran *ahead* of their polls by about 6 points.
- So the final polls are, if anything, *less* Democratic than the special-election signal. That is the reverse of 2022, when polls drifted red while the specials (Dobbs-era) stayed blue.

**Bottom line:**
- On the record, a 2020-size miss in a midterm would be unprecedented in the modern era. A 2–4 point Senate-level miss toward Republicans is entirely ordinary (Enten), and so is a miss toward Democrats (2025).
- Silver's model treats a miss in either direction as "roughly equally likely" (3 Oct).
- Cohn's last word: there is "no reason to assume that 2016 or 2020 is the worst-case scenario."

## 5. The two cases, as each side makes them

### (a) The Republican case for beating expectations

1. **The polls are broken again.** Silver's own framing: "Republicans' best hope is that the polls suck again." He notes that past errors hit the same working-class white voters Trump gained (Yahoo, 30 Oct). Supporting points:
   - RedState's Adam Turner (3 Nov) expects Democrats to be overstated by about 2 points nationally and more than 6 in Iowa, Michigan and Ohio.
   - Turner criticizes AtlasIntel's all-adults sample and Times/Siena's response rates.
   - Turner says GOP independents losing by 22 points is "unlikely".
   - Turner says early vote is "almost identical" to 2022.
   - The Washington Post Kansas nonresponse admission.
2. **Turnout, not persuasion.** NRCC chair Richard Hudson (Fox, 25 Oct): "The election will be determined based on who shows up… if Republican voters turn out, we're going to hold the House." He says about 30 seats are "virtually tied" and that Republicans have "the money", "better candidates" and new maps that make the battlefield "more favorable." Former GOP staffer John Fury: "This is not a persuasion election. This is a get-out-the base election" (Al Jazeera, 2 Nov).
3. **Redistricting:** a paper gain of roughly R+9 to R+10 (Wikipedia). Even a large popular-vote loss may yield only a modest Democratic majority (Dunham).
4. **The economy is stronger than in 2006.** Hewitt (3 Nov) compares 3.4% Q3 GDP with 1.6% in 2006. He argues voters will tie gas and inflation to a war the US is "decisively winning", and notes 30+ Trump October appearances against Bush's 16 in October 2006.
5. **Money and candidates:**
   - Republican super PACs put more than $150M into Texas for Paxton, versus about $4M for Talarico (Newsweek, 11 Oct).
   - Hewitt estimates about $4B in total spending with a GOP edge.
   - Hewitt attacks Democratic nominees Jackson, El-Sayed and Peltola as weak.
6. **The Democratic brand is weak.** Democratic party favorability is 44/55 (Fox) and 38/58 (WSJ) (Newsweek, 11 Oct). Former Democratic Senator Heidi Heitkamp: voters are "rejecting the party of Donald Trump," not choosing Democrats.
7. **Specific Senate paths** (Newsweek, 26 Oct):
   - Flip Michigan (Rogers).
   - Hold Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina.
   - A long-shot Minnesota play, where Steve Kornacki sees "a tide within the state that's working against Democrats" amid fraud scandals.
   - Marist's Lee Miringoff: "So many of the states are tossups."
8. **Early vote "spooky but not fatal" (DDHQ, 1 Nov):**
   - Post-COVID, Election Day voters lean Republican.
   - Iowa casts most of its votes on Election Day.
   - Florida shows a GOP early-vote lead.
   - Some Democratic early votes are simply cannibalized Election Day votes.

**Weak points in this case, for balance:**
- The Hispanic and young-male realignment looks to be reversing in 2026 polls. Axios (5 Oct) reports "Latino voters pull away from Trump and GOP"; CNBC (14 Oct) reports shifts in South Texas.
- Cook (28 Oct) says the seats with the most Trump-era swing (blue-collar, Hispanic, independent) have the most potential to "zoom back."
- Early-vote party splits are worse for Republicans than at this point in 2024 in North Carolina, Iowa, Kansas, Georgia and Texas (DDHQ).

### (b) The Democratic case for a wave

1. **Approval:** the second-lowest midterm approval since 1946. Inflation approval is 25/70.
2. **Specials:** an average overperformance of about 13 points (Downballot). Democrats improved their margin in every House special. There was a 25-point swing in GA-14. 2025 polls *underestimated* Democrats by 6.
3. **Enthusiasm:**
   - Reuters/Ipsos: 46% of Democrats vs 30% of Republicans are "very enthusiastic".
   - KFF: 63% vs 38% "more motivated".
   - Marist: 91% vs 86% enthusiastic.
4. **Prices, gas and tariffs:** CPI 3.7%, gas $4.29, about $1,100 per household in tariffs. Former Democratic staffer Eric Ham: Americans are paying more because of tariffs and "this war of choice" (Al Jazeera).
5. **Healthcare:** ACA subsidy expiry and an enrollment drop of more than 1 million. Healthcare is tied with the economy as the top issue in Gallup, and Democrats lead on it 42–22 (KFF).
6. **Iran war:** 47% call it extremely important (Gallup).
7. **Map expansion and party voices:**
   - DCCC chair Suzan DelBene: "The path to the majority… runs right through Pennsylvania" (Inquirer, 2 Nov).
   - Democrats cite "affordability, corruption, and the war in Iran" (Inquirer, 2 Nov).
   - Cook's Carrie Dann (27 Oct) on voter fury about corruption.
   - Kessler (Newsweek): "a gathering storm that could drown a lot of Republicans."
8. **Polls may be too cautious.** Times/Siena's old method was 3+ points bluer. Silver warns pollsters may have over-corrected.

**Democratic worries ("confident and paranoid", CNN, 1 Nov):**
- Barack Obama warned donors that 2–3 point leads are "within the margin of error" (Newsweek).
- Doug Gordon: "Every Democrat should be running as if they're 10 points behind."
- Axios (3 Nov): expectations are so high that "some Democrats fear disappointment is inevitable."

## 6. Historical context: president's party in midterms, 1994–2022

Source: American Presidency Project (UCSB), using Gallup late-October approval.

| Year | President | Approval | House | Senate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1994 | Clinton (D) | 48% | −54 | −8 |
| 1998 | Clinton (D) | 65% | +4 | 0 |
| 2002 | G.W. Bush (R) | 67% | +8 | +2 |
| 2006 | G.W. Bush (R) | 37% | −30 | −6 |
| 2010 | Obama (D) | 45% | −63 | −6 |
| 2014 | Obama (D) | 41% | −13 | −9 |
| 2018 | Trump (R) | 44% | −41 | +2 |
| 2022 | Biden (D) | 40% | −9 | +1 |
| **2026** | **Trump (R)** | **~37–39% (averages)** | Democrats need +3 | Democrats need +4 net |

What the table shows:
- Every president below 50% lost House seats.
- Senate results depend on the map. In 2018 Trump gained Senate seats while losing 41 House seats.
- 2026's approval sits with 2006 (Bush at 37%: −30 House, −6 Senate).
- 2022 shows a sub-45% president can lose only 9 seats when other factors (Dobbs, candidate quality) cut against the wave.
- Sabato's Crystal Ball (2026) argues approval movement on the path to the midterm shows "no pattern."
